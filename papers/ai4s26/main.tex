\begin{abstract}
Neural flow surrogates are usually compared by field error, although their
practical value often lies in the designs they select. We compare a
convolutional neural network (CNN) U-Net, a physics-regularized point
network, a Fourier neural operator (FNO), and a deep operator network
(DeepONet) on 381 OpenFOAM simulations of hydrofoils in water. Sixty-four cases
from two unseen NACA families form the validation set. The models predict
Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) fields, lift, drag, and pressure-based
cavitation-inception risk before driving the same four-parameter shape search.
The physics-regularized network gives the best pressure accuracy
($R^2=0.966$), cavitation F1 score (0.921), and inference speed; DeepONet gives
the most accurate force coefficients. Inference is 917--1801 times faster than
computational fluid dynamics (CFD). Fresh OpenFOAM simulations confirm
lift-to-drag ratio ($L/D$)
gains of 2.3--79.7\% for
the four selected designs, while a matched direct-CFD search reaches
$L/D=29.89$. Three surrogates select CFD-verified designs above that local-search
reference, with the best reaching 45.96. Field accuracy, force accuracy, and
optimization performance are related but not interchangeable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
For a hydrofoil operating in water, shape design is an optimization problem
wrapped around a flow solver. Each change in camber, thickness, or incidence
requires another Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) solution, so even a
modest search can consume hundreds of computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
evaluations; neural surrogates move most of that search off the solver by
learning the map from geometry and operating conditions to flow and forces
\cite{brunton2020machine}. The difficult question is not simply which network
minimizes field error, but which one preserves the quantities that determine a
useful design.

The convolutional neural network U-Net (CNN-U-Net), Fourier neural operator
(FNO), deep operator network (DeepONet), and physics-regularized point network
impose very different structure on that map
\cite{ronneberger2015unet,li2021fno,lu2021deeponet,raissi2019pinn}. A
convolutional model emphasizes local multiscale features; FNO mixes information
globally in Fourier space; DeepONet separates parameter and coordinate
representations; and a physics penalty constrains a pointwise regressor. Their
relative merits may therefore change as the target moves from pressure fields
to forces and finally to shape optimization.

Airfoil-surrogate research has progressed from field prediction
\cite{guo2016steady,bhatnagar2019aerodynamic,sekar2019airfoils,thuerey2020rans}
to benchmarks that evaluate surface forces, out-of-distribution accuracy, and
computational cost \cite{bonnet2022airfrans,li2023gino,yagoubi2025ml4cfd}.
Those metrics establish whether a model approximates CFD, but they do not by
themselves establish whether it will make the same design decision. A small,
structured force error can be amplified when an optimizer repeatedly queries a
narrow region of design space. The missing experiment is a controlled
comparison that follows several surrogate families through the same optimizer
and then checks the selected geometries with new CFD solutions. High-fidelity
endpoint verification is standard in surrogate shape optimization, while a matched direct-CFD
search provides a stronger reference when its cost is manageable
\cite{li2022mlaso,shukla2023operators}.

That experiment is the contribution of this paper; we do not propose a new
architecture. We compare four representative families on the same RANS data,
complete-geometry validation split, targets, and three-start hydrodynamic shape
optimization (HSO) problem. The
benchmark connects field and force accuracy, pressure-threshold cavitation
screening, and runtime to the final geometry selected by each model. Fresh
OpenFOAM solutions measure endpoint discrepancy, and a direct OpenFOAM search
uses the same starts and optimization budget. Together, these tests ask whether
validation accuracy predicts search quality and how closely each inexpensive
search approaches the CFD-driven result.

\section{Related Work}
Convolutional surrogates established that steady flow fields can be learned
directly from geometry encodings on Cartesian grids
\cite{guo2016steady,bhatnagar2019aerodynamic,thuerey2020rans}. Later datasets
and benchmarks broadened evaluation beyond image error: AIRFRANS includes
surface quantities and out-of-distribution splits, the geometric neural
operator (GINO) addresses irregular three-dimensional geometries, and ML4CFD evaluates modern operator-learning
systems under a common protocol
\cite{bonnet2022airfrans,li2023gino,yagoubi2025ml4cfd}. These studies motivate
our geometry-level validation split and separate reporting of fields, forces,
runtime, and low-pressure classification.

Neural operators represent an entire parameter-to-solution map rather than a
single flow realization. FNO performs global spectral mixing on a regular
grid, whereas DeepONet combines a parameter-dependent branch representation
with coordinate-dependent trunk functions
\cite{li2021fno,lu2021deeponet,kovachki2023neural}. Physics-informed learning
offers a different inductive bias by penalizing differential-equation
residuals \cite{raissi2019pinn,li2024pino}. The present comparison intentionally
includes both grid operators and pointwise models, since their relative value
may change when the output is used for optimization rather than visualization.

Learning-based airfoil optimization has shown large online speedups and has
validated selected optima with higher-fidelity solvers
\cite{li2022mlaso,shukla2023operators}. Most such studies develop or test one
surrogate family. Our contribution is complementary: data, split, targets,
optimizer, starts, bounds, and endpoint solver are held fixed while the model
family changes. A matched direct-CFD search then distinguishes prediction error
from the effect of optimizing a learned objective.

\section{Data and CFD Reference}
\subsection{Design space}
The dataset contains National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) 0006,
0008, 0010, 0012, 0015, 0018, 2412, 2415, 4412, 4415, 4418, and 4421 profiles
generated from the standard four-digit parameterization
\cite{abbott1959airfoils}. For each profile, we simulate angles of attack
$\alpha\in\{-4,-2,0,2,4,6,8,10\}^{\circ}$ and Reynolds numbers
$Re\in\{10^5,2\times10^5,5\times10^5,10^6\}$. All cases use liquid water
with density 997~kg/m$^3$ and kinematic viscosity $10^{-6}$~m$^2$/s. With a
1~m chord, the freestream speed is set by $U_\infty=Re\nu/c$; the far-field
absolute pressure and vapor pressure are 101325 and 2300~Pa.

Each case is solved with OpenFOAM \texttt{simpleFoam}
\cite{weller1998tensorial}. For steady incompressible flow, the mean velocity
$\bar{u}_i$ and pressure $\bar{p}$ satisfy
\begin{align}
\frac{\partial \bar{u}_i}{\partial x_i}&=0,\\
\rho\bar{u}_j\frac{\partial\bar{u}_i}{\partial x_j}
&=-\frac{\partial\bar{p}}{\partial x_i}
+\mu\frac{\partial^2\bar{u}_i}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}
-\rho\frac{\partial\overline{u_i'u_j'}}{\partial x_j}.
\end{align}
The Reynolds stress is closed with the $k$--$\omega$ shear-stress transport
(SST) model
\cite{menter1994sst}. Angled freestream velocity is imposed at the inlet and
far boundary, and OpenFOAM's \texttt{forceCoeffs} supplies total lift and drag
after 300 iterations. Cell-centered fields are interpolated to a common
$128\times64$ Cartesian grid over $x/c\in[-1,2]$ and
$y/c\in[-0.75,0.75]$. Inputs include coordinates, operating parameters, three
NACA descriptors, a fluid mask, and signed distance to the profile.

Three simulations with $|C_L|$ or $|C_D|>5$ are discarded before splitting,
leaving 381 cases. All 64 cases from NACA 0015 and NACA 2412 form the
validation set; the remaining 317 cases train the models. Because complete
families are reserved, neither validation geometry appears in training at a
different operating condition.
All retained solves reach iteration 300. At the final iteration, the
95th-percentile maximum initial equation residual is $1.27\times10^{-4}$; over
the last 50 iterations, the 95th-percentile spans are 0.0059 for $C_L$ and
$2.65\times10^{-4}$ for $C_D$.

\subsection{Targets and cavitation-risk definition}
The common output vector contains $U_x$, $U_y$, absolute pressure $p$, eddy
viscosity, $k$, $\omega$, pressure coefficient $C_p$, cavitation margin, lift
coefficient $C_L$, and drag coefficient $C_D$. Grid fields use masked
normalized mean-squared error, while the case-level force targets use
normalized mean-squared error. OpenFOAM's kinematic gauge pressure $p_k$ is
converted to absolute pressure as
\begin{equation}
 p=p_\infty+\rho p_k, \qquad C_p=\frac{p-p_\infty}{\tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2}.
\end{equation}
For an ambient-pressure value $p_a$, the pressure-based inception margin is
\begin{equation}
 M(x,y;p_a)=p_a+C_p(x,y)\tfrac{1}{2}\rho U_\infty^2-p_v.
\end{equation}
A cell is labeled at risk when $M<0$. We evaluate this label at $p_a\in\{2500,3000,5000,10000,25000,101325\}$~Pa. This sweep creates a useful classification test from the predicted $C_p$ field without representing multiphase physics.

\section{Surrogates and Optimization}
\subsection{Model families}
The \textbf{CNN-U-Net} has three encoder/decoder levels, skip connections,
group normalization, Gaussian error linear unit (GELU) activations, and base
width 16. It maps the complete
input grid to the output grid \cite{ronneberger2015unet}. The \textbf{FNO} has width 32, four operator
blocks, and 12 retained Fourier modes in each direction. One block is
\begin{equation}
h_{\ell+1}=\sigma\!\left(W_\ell h_\ell+
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(R_\ell\mathcal{F}(h_\ell)\right)\right).
\end{equation}
following the spectral operator construction of \cite{li2021fno}.
Here, $W_\ell$ is a local linear map and $R_\ell$ acts on the retained spectral
modes. The \textbf{DeepONet} uses three-layer, width-64 branch and trunk
networks with 64 basis functions. For output $k$,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{y}_k(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu})=
\frac{\mathbf{b}_k(\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\mathbf{t}_k(\mathbf{x})}{\sqrt{64}}+c_k,
\end{equation}
as in the branch--trunk construction of \cite{lu2021deeponet},
where $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ contains the operating and geometry parameters.

The \textbf{physics-regularized multilayer perceptron (MLP)} has three tanh
layers of width 64. It is
supervised by CFD values at 1,024 sampled points per case. Its extra loss uses
the directional residuals
\begin{equation}
r_c=u_x+v_y,\quad r_x=u u_x+p_x/\rho,\quad r_y=v v_y+p_y/\rho,
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\rm sup}+10^{-4}\mathcal{L}_{\rm phys}$,
following the residual-loss principle in \cite{raissi2019pinn}.
This is a lightweight physics regularizer, not a data-free physics-informed
neural network (PINN) or a complete RANS residual.

Table~\ref{tab:architectures} summarizes the resulting inductive biases. The
two grid models consume the entire Cartesian field at once. DeepONet and the
physics-regularized MLP instead evaluate coordinates, which makes their
representation naturally pointwise, although the present timing benchmark
requests the same complete grid from every model.

Optimization uses dedicated $C_L$ and $C_D$ output heads supervised by

\subsection{Why physics regularization rather than a full PINN?}
A classical PINN is trained by differentiating its outputs and minimizing the
governing-equation and boundary-condition residuals, sometimes with few or no
solution labels \cite{raissi2019pinn}. Our point network remains primarily
supervised by OpenFOAM values; its low-weight residual contains continuity and
inviscid directional momentum only. It omits viscous stress, Reynolds stress,
the $k$--$\omega$ transport equations, and explicit wall and far-field
residuals. Describing it as a full RANS PINN would therefore overstate the
physics imposed during training.

This lighter comparator was chosen because it applies a recognizable physical
bias while retaining the same labels, split, and modest training budget as the
other surrogates. A full hydrofoil RANS PINN is a different experiment: it
would require coupled turbulence variables and boundary losses, careful
residual balancing near the wall, and substantially more automatic-
differentiation work. The label ``physics-regularized MLP'' keeps that
distinction visible when interpreting its strong pressure results.

\subsection{Common optimization protocol}
At $Re=5\times10^5$, each surrogate drives the same HSO loop. The design
vector $\theta=(m,p,t,\alpha)$ contains maximum camber, camber location,
thickness, and incidence, with bounds $m\in[0,0.09]$, $p\in[0.1,0.9]$,
$t\in[0.06,0.20]$, and $\alpha\in[-4,10]^{\circ}$. We launch Nelder--Mead
\cite{nelder1965simplex} from NACA 0012, 2412, and 4415 and give every run the
same 40-iteration budget. The objective is
\begin{equation}
J(\theta)=\frac{C_L}{|C_D|+10^{-6}}-10P_M-5f_M,
\end{equation}
where $P_M$ penalizes a predicted minimum cavitation margin below 5000~Pa and
$f_M$ is the fraction of cells below that margin. The search calls only the
surrogate; OpenFOAM is not used inside the loop. Since the nominal-pressure
margin stays above the threshold for every selected design, the penalty is
inactive in this experiment. We return the best design observed within the
fixed budget, select the strongest of the three starts for each architecture,
and then generate a new mesh and independent OpenFOAM simulation for that
winner.

For the direct-CFD baseline, the same Nelder--Mead procedure starts from the
same three foils and uses the same bounds, objective, tolerances, and
40-iteration budget, but every evaluation is obtained by remeshing and running
OpenFOAM. This separates endpoint verification, which checks each surrogate's
winner once, from CFD-driven optimization, which supplies a matched reference
search.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Validation accuracy and computational cost}
Table~\ref{tab:performance} reports results on the 64-case validation set. The
physics-regularized network is best for pressure and $C_p$, with a pressure
root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 6.78~Pa and $R^2=0.966$. DeepONet gives the
best $U_x$, $U_y$, $C_L$, and $C_D$ coefficients. The grid operators are
weaker in this low-data setting. FNO retains useful force trends. CNN-U-Net
predicts $C_p$ and forces more accurately than absolute pressure or streamwise
velocity. The normalized multi-target loss and the affine relationship between
$p$ and $C_p$ allow this difference between channels.

\subsection{Cavitation-inception screening}
The pressure-threshold labels are highly imbalanced, so accuracy is not useful here. The physics-regularized network achieves precision 0.927, recall 0.916, and F1 0.921. DeepONet follows at F1 0.913. CNN-U-Net has the highest recall (0.958) but lower precision (0.796), while FNO obtains F1 0.771. Twenty-four validation case/ambient-pressure combinations contain at-risk cells. This test measures whether a surrogate preserves low-$C_p$ regions well enough for inception screening. It does not model cavity dynamics. That would require multiphase RANS labels such as vapor volume fraction and cavity length \cite{brennen1995cavitation}.

\section{Discussion}
DeepONet's 2.1\% endpoint error confirms that force calibration carries into
HSO, but it does not find the best design: the physics-regularized network
reaches $L/D=45.96$ rather than 40.86. CNN-U-Net and FNO both underpredict their
selected endpoints by about 20\%, yet only CNN-U-Net locates a substantial
gain. Candidate ranking and value calibration are therefore distinct model
properties.

The direct-CFD history sharpens that distinction. It spends 266 evaluations
and 38.5 minutes of summed wall time, yet its best result is $L/D=29.89$.
Endpoint-validated CNN-U-Net, DeepONet, and physics-regularized designs exceed
that value by 8.56, 10.97, and 16.07, respectively. The direct run is a matched
local-search baseline, not a claim of the global CFD optimum; its value is that
it isolates how the evaluator changes search behavior at fixed settings.

The three starts also expose different objective landscapes. The
physics-regularized model predicts nearly identical optima from NACA 2412 and
4415 ($L/D=43.55$ and 43.62) but only 22.35 from NACA 0012; FNO remains between
16.18 and 21.11 across all starts. The three stronger endpoint designs approach
6\% thickness, whereas FNO selects 16.8\%. All winners retain about 99 kPa of
nominal predicted cavitation margin, so the penalty is inactive: the present
HSO result is an $L/D$ experiment accompanied by cavitation screening, not a
demonstration that cavitation changes the optimum.

Table~\ref{tab:allstarts} also shows why reporting only one optimization start
would be misleading. Three models and direct CFD favor a cambered start, but
the preferred family differs, and the spread across starts ranges from 4.94
for FNO to 21.27 for the physics-regularized MLP. Because these are predictions
on different learned objectives, their magnitudes are not a cross-model
accuracy ranking. They quantify search sensitivity within each evaluator; the
fresh CFD endpoint values in Table~\ref{tab:optimization} remain the common
comparison.

The field results help explain why parameter count alone is not predictive.
The 9,930-parameter physics-regularized MLP is the smallest model and gives the
best pressure result, while the 595,914-parameter FNO is the largest and ranks
third on pressure. In this dataset, an operator model receives only 317
complete training realizations even though each realization contains many grid
cells. The pointwise networks turn those same cases into supervised coordinate
samples and encode the operating parameters directly. This is favorable for a
fixed grid and a low number of geometries, but it does not imply that pointwise
models will dominate as geometric diversity, resolution, or three-dimensional
domain size grows. The comparison instead identifies the inductive bias that
works best at the present data scale.

Runtime should likewise be interpreted at the workflow level. Repeating the
266 evaluations used by the matched direct search would require about
38.5 minutes of CFD wall time, compared with approximately 1.8--3.6 seconds of
surrogate inference. Even including training, the measured online cost breaks
even after roughly 5 DeepONet, 9 physics-regularized MLP, 45 FNO, or 71
CNN-U-Net evaluations. Those counts exclude CFD data generation, which is the
dominant up-front expense and must be amortized across operating sweeps or
multiple design studies. The speedups therefore support reuse of a trained
surrogate, not replacement of the simulations needed to construct it.

The water operating conditions also clarify the cavitation result. At
$Re=5\times10^5$ and the stated 1~m chord, $U_\infty=0.5$~m/s and the dynamic
pressure is only about 125~Pa. With nominal ambient pressure near 101~kPa, none
of the optimized designs approaches vapor pressure, so the cavitation penalty
cannot influence the optimum. The lower-pressure sweep tests whether the
networks preserve the leading-edge low-pressure pattern under an inception
criterion; it should not be interpreted as a prediction of a developed vapor
cavity. A future cavitation-constrained design study would choose operating
pressure and velocity jointly so that inception is active within the search
space.

Finally, three surrogate winners approach the lower thickness bound. This is a
common place for learned-objective error to matter because the optimizer is
querying continuous shapes that were not simulated during training. The fresh
mesh-and-solve endpoint check answers the immediate question---whether the
selected shape still performs in the reference solver---without claiming that
the learned surface is uniformly accurate around that point. Local CFD samples
around each winner, structural thickness constraints, or a trust-region update
would be natural additions when the objective is a deployable design rather
than the controlled benchmark considered here.

The comparison uses one training seed, two-dimensional steady RANS, and the
NACA four-digit space. A formal grid-convergence study, external force
validation, and repeated training seeds remain the highest-value checks.

\section{Conclusion}
We followed four neural surrogates through the complete path from RANS-field
prediction to a design decision. On unseen NACA families, the
physics-regularized point network gives the best pressure result
($R^2=0.966$) and cavitation-inception F1 score (0.921), while DeepONet gives
the most accurate lift and drag. Every model evaluates a pressure field in
6.9--13.5~ms, corresponding to 917--1801$\times$ speedup over the measured
OpenFOAM runs.

The HSO experiment changes the ranking from a simple accuracy table. DeepONet
predicts its winner most accurately, but the physics-regularized network finds
the best design, increasing OpenFOAM $L/D$ from 25.57 to 45.96. CNN-U-Net and
DeepONet also find substantial gains, from 25.89 to 38.45 and 40.86,
respectively, whereas FNO reaches only 26.15 from 25.57. The matched direct-CFD
search reaches 29.89, showing that a fast surrogate can change not only search
cost but also which basin a budgeted local optimizer reaches. A useful scientific
surrogate should therefore be judged on both its validation predictions and
the consequence of acting on them. In this benchmark, the strongest model is
not merely the one with the lowest aggregate loss; it is the one that combines
accurate pressure screening, fast evaluation, and reliable guidance inside the
design loop.
